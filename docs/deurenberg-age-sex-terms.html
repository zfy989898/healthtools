The Deurenberg age and sex terms

A method note for src/tools/body-fat-from-bmi-calculator.mjs, which implements the body-fat prediction equation published by Deurenberg, Weststrate and Seidell in 1991. The calculator itself is on the live site at healthtools.icu/body-fat-from-bmi-calculator/; this page covers only what the two correction terms do and where the equation stops being supported by its own data.

The equation as the module implements it

BF% = 1.20 x BMI + 0.23 x age - 10.8 x sex - 5.4, where BMI = weight in kg / (height in m)^2 and sex is coded 1 for male, 0 for female

Read as code, the four constants are a BMI term, an age term, a sex term and a flat subtraction. The module keeps them as named values rather than inlining them, so each figure from the paper appears exactly once.

TermCoefficientWhat it does to the answer
BMI1.2Every point of BMI adds 1.2 points of estimated body fat.
Age0.23Height and weight held constant, the estimate rises 10.4 points between 20 and 65 years.
Sex10.8 for males, 0 for females, then a flat 5.4At identical age, height and weight the two sexes differ by exactly 10.8 points, because the sex term is the only one that switches.

The age term is the reason a calculator of this type cannot be a body-fat measurement. It is a linear stand-in for the loss of lean tissue that tracks age in a population average, applied to whoever types in a number.

At the same BMI and age the two sexes are never given the same answer, and the gap is the 10.8 points of the sex term. The comparison that trips people up runs the other way: an older man against a younger woman at one BMI, where the age term climbs while the sex term does not, so the two almost meet. At BMI 25 this module returns 28.7 for a 65-year-old man and 29.2 for a 20-year-old woman. Two figures that close are a coincidence of the arithmetic, not a claim that the two bodies are similar.

The range the published fit covers

The equation was derived from a sample measured by densitometry and validated inside the observed spread of that data. What the source paper states, and what the module therefore encodes as limits:

ReportedValueHow the module uses it
Standard error of the estimate4.1 points of body fatShown to the user as the interval around the point figure, not hidden.
FitR-squared 0.79Quoted on the page; the residual fifth of variation is what the terms cannot see.
BMI span of the sample13.9 to 40.9 kg/m2Outside it, the page flags the result as an extrapolation.
Age span of the sample7 to 83 yearsThe input floor is 16, because the paediatric equation is separate.

The same paper gives a second equation for those aged 15 and under, BF% = 1.51 x BMI - 0.70 x age - 3.6 x sex + 1.4 (aged 15 and under, R-squared 0.38, SEE 4.4% body fat). It is a weaker model, and this module refuses to apply it: a formula fitted on growing bodies is a different measurement problem. The authors also report that in obese subjects the prediction formulas slightly overestimate body fat, so the error is likelier to grow towards the bottom of the grid than to sit evenly across it.

Why this page does not rank you against anyone

A body-fat percentage from BMI is one of several estimates of the same quantity that disagree by points, and a difference smaller than the standard error is not a finding. The sibling calculator on this site, the US Navy circumference method, uses a tape measure instead of age and sex, so the two can be read against each other as two different questions. Selection rules for which figure gets published at all are on the site's methodology page.
